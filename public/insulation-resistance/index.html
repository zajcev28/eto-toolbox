    How the reading is evaluated

    Test voltage. For cables and circuits the usual marine practice (IEC 60092-401 and class rules) is twice the rated voltage for circuits up to 250 V, 500 V DC for 250–1000 V, 1000 V DC up to 7.2 kV and 5000 V DC above that. For rotating machines the calculator follows IEEE 43: 500 V DC for windings below 1 kV, rising to 5–10 kV for high-voltage machines.

    Minimum value. Cables and circuits rated up to 1000 V should show at least 1 MΩ; above 1000 V the minimum is (rated kV + 1) MΩ. For motors and generators IEEE 43 recommends at least 5 MΩ at 40 °C for random-wound and LV windings, and 100 MΩ for form-wound windings. These are minimums for putting equipment into service, not targets: a healthy installation normally reads far higher.

    Temperature correction. Insulation resistance roughly halves for every 10 °C rise, so readings from a cold machine look better than they are. Machine readings are corrected to 40 °C:

    R40 = Rt × 0.5^((40 − t) / 10)

    Cable readings are shown as measured. Correction factors for cables depend on the insulation material, so compare cable readings taken at similar temperatures.

    PI and DAR. The polarization index (10 min ÷ 1 min) and dielectric absorption ratio (1 min ÷ 30 s) show whether the insulation is dry and clean. A rising reading during the test is healthy; a flat or falling one points to moisture or contamination. IEEE 43 recommends a PI of at least 2.0 for class B, F and H insulation. When the 1-minute reading is above about 5000 MΩ, PI is not a reliable indicator.

    Trend. The most useful check on board is comparison with your own log. A drop of more than half since the last test, at a comparable temperature, is worth investigating even if the value is still above the minimum.

    Before you megger

    Isolate and lock out the circuit, prove it dead, and disconnect anything electronic that the test voltage can damage: drives, AVRs, PLC I/O, sensors, surge suppressors. After the test, discharge the cable or winding to earth; long cables and large machines can hold a dangerous charge for minutes.

    FAQ

    My motor reads 2 MΩ cold. Can I start it?

    Corrected to 40 °C it will be lower still, below the IEEE 43 recommended minimum. Find the cause first: moisture is the usual suspect after a long standstill, and drying out with anti-condensation heaters or low-voltage heating often brings it back.

    Why is my PI below 1?

    The reading fell during the test, which usually means moisture, contamination or a leakage path. Treat it as a fault indication and investigate before running the machine.

    Does 1 MΩ mean a cable is fine?

    It is the acceptance floor, not a healthy value. A sound LV cable usually reads hundreds of MΩ or more. If a circuit sits just above 1 MΩ, it needs attention.
