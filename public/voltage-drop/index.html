How the calculation works

The calculator first works out the load current, then checks every standard cross-section from 1.5 to 300 mm² against two conditions. The recommended size is the smallest one that meets both.

1. Current-carrying capacity. Base ampacities are for copper cables with 90 °C insulation at 45 °C ambient, the reference ambient for engine rooms. They are corrected for the number of cores (2-core ×0.85, 3/4-core ×0.70), for ambient temperature, and for bunching where selected.

2. Voltage drop.

3-phase: ΔU = √3 · I · L · (R·cos φ + X·sin φ) 1-phase: ΔU = 2 · I · L · (R·cos φ + X·sin φ) DC: ΔU = 2 · I · L · R

L is the one-way length. R is the conductor resistance corrected to its estimated operating temperature, which depends on how heavily the cable is loaded. With parallel cables the current is shared equally between them.

What voltage drop is allowed on a ship?

IEC 60092-352 is the usual reference: under normal conditions the drop from the main or emergency switchboard to any point of the installation should not exceed 6% of nominal voltage, and up to 10% is commonly allowed for battery supplies of 50 V or less. Motor starting, navigation lights and some essential consumers can have their own limits, and classification societies may be stricter, so check the rules that apply to your vessel.

FAQ

Why does a bigger cable sometimes still fail?

Long runs on low voltages are limited by voltage drop rather than heating. Try parallel cables or check whether the consumer can be supplied from a closer distribution board.

Should I enter the one-way or the total length?

One-way: the distance from the switchboard to the consumer. The formulas already account for the return path.

Can I use this for final design?

Use it for quick checks and comparisons. For new installations, confirm against the cable manufacturer's data, the applicable standard and your class society's rules.
